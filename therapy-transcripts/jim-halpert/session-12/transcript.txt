clinician: Hi Jim. How was the holding?

client: The not-acting. The "hold it, don't act" is getting harder. I know I'm with Karen because she's safe. I know I'm in love with Pam. I know I'm hovering. I know all of it. clinician: So you want to move from knowing to doing.
client: I want to. But the doing is — Pam is engaged. I can't tell Pam while she's with Roy. And I can't leave Karen without a reason. And the reason is "I'm in love with someone else." client: Pam → I have feelings → feelings mean wanting → wanting means mattering → mattering means I can lose → losing means feeling → feeling means being on the ground → being on the ground means being vulnerable → being vulnerable means being my father → being my father means the chair → the chair means disappearing. That's the chain. Pam all the way to disappearing. Every feeling is a straight line to my father's chair. And the hover interrupts the line at "feelings" and redirects to "joke" and the joke cuts the circuit.
clinician: And the design project? The father call? Those were on the ground. Did they lead to the chair?
client: No. They didn't. The design project led to satisfaction. The phone call led to four minutes of ordinary. Neither one led to the chair. The chain is broken. The "feelings → chair" line is wrong. I've been treating it as a law of physics — feelings lead to chair, always, without exception. But the evidence says otherwise. The design project had feelings and no chair. The phone call had feelings and no chair. The law is wrong.

clinician: Are you ready to jump?

clinician: A step, not a fall. That's the goal. Not jumping — stepping.
client: Stepping. Not jumping. That's — I like that. Jumping is the emergency. Stamford was a jump. Stepping is the small landing applied to Pam. One step. One honest sentence. Not "I love you." Just "I'm not over it."
