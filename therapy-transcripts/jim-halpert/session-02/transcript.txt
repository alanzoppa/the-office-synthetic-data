clinician: Hi Jim. How was the week?
client: Fine. Normal. Same as always. Work, basketball, TV. The usual.
clinician: You said "fine" again. Last week you told me "fine" is a shield.
client: I did say that. [pause] Okay. Not fine. I don't know how the week was. I don't — I never know how the week was because I don't pay attention to how the week is while it's happening. I'm just — in it. And then it's over. And I say "fine" because I don't have a better word.
clinician: Let's try something. Instead of "fine," describe the week as a place. Where were you this week?
client: A place. [pause] I was in a waiting room. Like a doctor's office waiting room. Where you sit and read old magazines and wait for your name to be called. I'm always in the waiting room. Waiting for — I don't know what. For something to start. For the real thing to begin. I've been in the waiting room for years.
clinician: What's the real thing?
client: I don't know. That's the problem. I don't know what I'm waiting for. I'm just — I'm in the holding pattern. I'm holding. And holding feels safe. Because if you're holding, you're not committed. You're not in anything. You're just — hovering.
clinician: Hovering. Above what?
client: Above everything. Above the job. Above — relationships. Above life. I'm hovering above my own life and watching it happen and not being in it. And it's — it's safe up there. Nothing can reach you when you're hovering. But nothing reaches you either. No up, no down. Just — floating.
clinician: You're describing something that sounds very lonely, Jim.
client: [pause] Yeah. It is lonely. I don't — I don't think about it as lonely because I'm around people all day. I'm at the office, I'm with people, I'm talking, I'm joking. But I'm hovering above it the whole time. And at the end of the day, I go home and I'm alone and the hovering is the loudest. Because there's no one to distract me from the floating.
clinician: You transferred to Stamford a while ago. From Scranton. What was that about?
client: I — it was a transfer. Corporate. Stamford branch was —
clinician: Jim. What was it really about?
client: [long pause] Pam. It was about Pam. I left because of Pam.
clinician: Tell me about Pam.
client: Pam is — Pam is the receptionist. She's my friend. We sit near each other. We talk all day. She's funny. Not performatively funny. Quiet funny. The kind of funny where you have to be paying attention to catch it. And I was — I was paying attention. And somewhere along the way, paying attention turned into — into feelings. And she was engaged. To Roy. Who she's been with since high school. And I couldn't — I couldn't be there and have feelings for someone who was engaged to someone else. So I left.
clinician: You left.
client: I left. I transferred to Stamford and I thought, "I'll start over. New office, new people, new Jim. A Jim who doesn't have feelings for someone he can't have." And it worked. For a while. I was in Stamford and I was — I was fine. Actually fine, not shield-fine. I didn't think about Pam every day. I dated. I had friends. I was — I was a version of myself that wasn't orbiting Pam.
clinician: And?
client: And then Stamford closed. And they sent me back to Scranton. And Pam was there. And Roy was there. And I was back in the same chair at the same desk and it was like I never left. Everything I ran from was right there. Waiting. Like it hadn't moved. Like it knew I'd come back.
clinician: What did you run from, Jim? Not Pam specifically — what did the Pam situation represent?
client: [pause] Wanting something I can't have. Caring about something that can't — that can't happen. And the caring — the caring felt like a trap. Because if I stayed and kept caring and kept pretending I didn't care, I'd be stuck. Stuck wanting. Stuck hovering. Stuck in the waiting room but with a name on the door. And I couldn't — I couldn't be stuck. So I ran.
clinician: You described hovering as safe. But you also described running from being stuck. Those seem contradictory. Which is it — do you avoid by hovering, or do you avoid by running?
client: Both. I hover when staying feels dangerous. And I run when hovering isn't enough. Hovering is the day-to-day. Running is the emergency protocol. When the hovering stops working — when something gets too close, too real — I run. Stamford was running. The transfer was the emergency protocol.
clinician: So the pattern is: you hover above your life, and when something threatens to pull you down into it, you run.
client: That's — yeah. That's the pattern. Hover and run. Hover and run. And I've been doing it since — I don't know. Since college? Since before college? I don't remember a time when I wasn't hovering. When I was actually in my life, on the ground, in it.
clinician: What would it feel like to be on the ground? In it?
client: Terrifying. Because being in it means — being in it means you can get hurt. When you're hovering, nothing touches you. When you're on the ground, everything touches you. Every conversation, every relationship, every choice. It all matters. And mattering is — mattering is the most dangerous thing I can think of.
clinician: Why?
client: Because if things matter, then losing them matters. And if losing things matters, then I have to feel the loss. And I don't — I don't do loss. I do "fine." I do "it's okay." I do "I'll move on." I do the shrug. The Jim Halpert shrug. "It's fine. It's okay. I'll get over it." And the shrug is the — it's the escape hatch. When something matters, I shrug it into not mattering. And then I don't have to feel it.
clinician: The shrug is the hovering in miniature.
client: The shrug is the hovering in miniature. Yeah. Every shrug is a small departure. A small "I'm leaving this before it can leave me." [pause] I've been shrugging my way through my whole life.
clinician: That's a lot to see in a second session, Jim.
client: I see things fast. I've always been quick. The problem isn't seeing — it's that seeing doesn't change anything. I can see the pattern and still do the pattern. I can name the trap and still be in it. Seeing is not escaping.
clinician: No. But it's the first step. You can't leave a room you don't know you're in.
client: I know I'm in the room. I've known for years. I just can't find the door.
clinician: Maybe the door isn't the problem. Maybe you're the one who keeps closing it.
client: [long pause] Yeah. Maybe. Maybe I'm the one closing it. I hadn't thought about it that way. I thought the door was hidden. But maybe it's right there and I keep shutting it. Because if the door is open, I have to walk through it. And walking through means being on the other side. And being on the other side means being in it.
clinician: Being in it.
client: Being in it. [pause] Okay. I'll come back next week. Not because I want to be in it. But because I'm tired of hovering. I'm tired of the waiting room. And maybe — maybe this room is the first room where I can practice being on the ground.
clinician: This room is a good place to practice.
client: Yeah. Okay. See you next week, Doc.
clinician: See you next week, Jim.
