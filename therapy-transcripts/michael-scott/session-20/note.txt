Subjective

Second treatment plan review conducted at the start of session. Clinician assessed revised goals:

**Goal 1 (Awareness):** Achieved and exceeded. Client has moved from real-time awareness to metacognitive awareness — can see the performance running even in contexts where he believed it was off (including therapy). No further active work needed.

**Goal 2 (Tolerate presence without performing):** Substantially achieved. Five behavioral experiments completed. Client can sit in unscripted silence, set boundaries in real time (Dwight, Jan, clinician), and has generalized the no-performance practice to multiple relationships.

**Goal 3 (Personal needs/identity):** This is the focus for the remaining five sessions. Client cannot yet describe himself outside of his occupational role. "I'm Michael Scott, regional manager. That's not just my job. That's my whole... I'm the boss." When asked what's left without the boss identity: "The condo. The couch. The cereal. The TV. The phone calls to Dwight. That's a waiting room. It's where I store myself until the next performance."

**Goal 4 (Attachment/Jan):** Understanding is strong and stable. Client reports the "staying might be self-abandonment" awareness has gotten clearer but has not yet led to action. "I'm not ready to do anything about it yet. But I see it. I see it clearly." Clinician assessed: appropriate stage. Action not expected before termination.

Identity exploration during this session yielded important discoveries:
- **Music/mix CDs:** Client makes mix CDs for himself that he never shares. During this private activity, "the songs do the feeling for me. I don't have to perform. I just ride the music." Identified as a receiving practice.
- **Night walks:** Client sometimes walks alone at night without calling anyone or listening to anything. "I'm just a guy walking. Not a boss. Not a performer. Just legs moving. And the night. And me."
- Both activities are genuine (non-performed) and represent seeds of identity outside the occupational role.

Key reframe: Client discovered that "just" — previously experienced as an insult ("you're just a salesman") — now feels sufficient. "Just is enough. Just Michael. Just showing up. Just when it comes." Clinician noted: "That might be the most important thing you've said in twenty weeks."
