Subjective

Client completed the "sitting with the feeling" homework three times during the week. He reported decreasing intensity of the "nobody's coming" feeling with each practice: first session ~40 seconds of acute discomfort before the feeling dulled; second session ~20 seconds; third session he was able to "greet" the feeling ("Oh, there you are") and coexist with it. Client framed this as learning to distinguish between a feeling and a threat: "The feeling says 'nobody's coming,' but that doesn't mean nobody is actually coming."

The session focused on building a systematic trigger map. Client identified:

1. **David Wallace (CFO):** Highest-intensity trigger. Client performs to secure approval because David controls the branch's survival. Feeling: "terror" — if David doesn't like him, the branch closes and everyone leaves.
2. **Jan:** Constant performance mode. Client describes the relationship as "a permanent audition." He performs to stay ahead of criticism and hides distress to avoid giving Jan "more material." Feeling: "exhausted and scared" — if the audition ends, she leaves or gives "that look."
3. **Toby (HR):** Unique trigger — the one person client does NOT perform for. Instead, Toby triggers anger. Client connected this to his stepfather Jeff: both are present but unresponsive, and unresponsiveness triggers the "nobody's coming" rejection feeling. With Jeff, client stopped trying; with Toby, he can't escape, so anger replaces performance as the defense.
4. **The condo at night:** Loneliness trigger. Only Dwight is called in crisis. Jan is called only when things are good — client hides pain from her to prevent criticism.

Assessment

The trigger map session was highly productive. The Toby-Jeff connection is a significant clinical development: client recognized that his anger toward Toby is a defense against the same rejection feeling that his stepfather's disengagement produced. This is the first time the client has identified an active present-day relationship that replays the childhood dynamic, and the first time he's identified anger (rather than performance) as an alternative defense.

The Jan material is becoming clearer as clinically concerning. Client describes a "permanent audition" and hides distress to avoid criticism. He frames this as normal but the pattern — concealing vulnerability, performing to prevent rejection, tolerating criticism to maintain proximity — maps directly onto the attachment schema. This warrants focused exploration in upcoming sessions.

The sitting practice is working: client is building distress tolerance for the core affective state. This supports Goal 2 (tolerating presence without performing).
