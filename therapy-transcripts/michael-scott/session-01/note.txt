Date: Week 1
Clinician: Dr. Karen Mitchell, LCSW
Client: Michael Scott
Session Type: Individual Therapy (Intake)

Subjective

Client self-referred for "work stress" and sleep disturbance. He presented as gregarious, talkative, and highly performative throughout the session, frequently launching into jokes, impressions, and stories when direct questions about his emotional life arose. He works as the regional manager of Dunder Mifflin Scranton branch and identifies strongly with his professional role.

Presenting complaints:
- Chronic sleep disruption (waking at approximately 3:00 AM, 4-5 nights per week)
- Racing thoughts at night focused on whether employees are happy and whether he handled situations correctly during the day
- Self-described "work stress" framed as an occupational hazard of management

When asked about relationships, client described his girlfriend Jan (formerly his corporate superior) as "smart" and noted she "has opinions about things, about me, about how I do things." He framed her criticism as supportive. He described his social life primarily through work relationships, identifying Dwight (Assistant to the Regional Manager) as the person he would call in a crisis. He could not name a friend outside of work when asked directly.

When asked about family, client stated "we're all fine" multiple times. He described his stepfather Jeff as someone who was physically present but emotionally disengaged ("we didn't really connect"). His biological father left when he was young; he redirected quickly when this topic arose. His mother was described as "loving" but "worked a lot." The word "fine" was used at least seven times in reference to family.

Notable observations:
- Client referred to himself as "a people person" and "a storyteller" — both performance-based identities
- Client revealed he gives himself awards (the "Dundies," an office awards ceremony he created, and a "World's Best Boss" mug he purchased for himself)
- When asked what he needs for himself, client redirected to what he provides for others
- When asked about his biological father leaving, client said "it's fine" and "it was a long time ago"

Notable quote: "I need them to be happy. That's what I need."

Objective

- Appearance: Well-groomed, business casual, energetic
- Behavior: Cooperative, talkative, prone to tangential storytelling when anxious; performed voices and impressions
- Mood: "Good" (client's word)
- Affect: Bright and performed; notable constriction when family topics arose, quickly redirected to humor
- Speech: Rapid, animated, circumstantial; difficult to interrupt during monologues
- Thought process: Linear when focused, but highly avoidant via humor and storytelling
- Insight: Limited — attributes distress to external stressors; does not connect sleep disturbance to interpersonal patterns
- Judgment: Intact
- Safety: Denies SI/HI

Assessment

Initial presentation consistent with chronic hyperarousal and a pervasive pattern of performance-based self-worth regulation. Client's "work stress" appears to be a surface presentation of deeper patterns around approval-seeking, identity fusion with occupational role, and emotional avoidance through humor.

Key clinical observations:
1. Performance as default: Client maintained a performative interpersonal stance throughout intake, including jokes, voices, and self-referential humor. Performance intensified when questions touched on family, personal needs, or vulnerability.
2. "Fine" as deflection: Repeated use of "fine" to characterize family relationships, particularly the stepfather and absent biological father, suggests significant avoidant attachment patterns.
3. Self-worth externalization: Client's self-worth appears contingent on being liked by employees, confirmed by authority figures (David Wallace), and maintained through self-generated validation (Dundies, self-purchased mug).
4. Caregiving as self-neglect: Client articulated a clear understanding of what each employee "needs" but could not identify his own needs when asked directly. His stated need — "I need them to be happy" — frames caregiving as self-care.
5. Relationship concerns: Client's relationship with Jan appears to involve significant criticism directed at him, which he frames as supportive. This warrants further exploration.

Working hypothesis: Client has developed a survival strategy of manufacturing approval and connection through performance, originating in childhood attachment experiences (emotionally disengaged stepfather, absent biological father, loving but frequently absent mother). This strategy has become ego-syntonic — client views performance as identity ("that's just who I am") rather than as a defense. The sleep disturbance is likely a symptom of nighttime being the only period when the performance is offline and underlying anxiety about approval and abandonment surfaces.

Plan

1. Accept "work stress and sleep" as the entry point without challenging the framing prematurely
2. Build alliance through demonstrating that the therapist can hold steady without being entertained — modeling that relationship does not require performance
3. Track the pattern of deflecting to humor/storytelling when vulnerable topics arise
4. Note the "fine" pattern around family and begin gentle mapping of family dynamics
5. Explore the Jan relationship more fully as sessions progress
6. Next session: Continue assessment; begin exploring the "need to be liked" as a possible internal rule
7. Client expressed strong positive feelings about the session ("you get me") — note this as possible early idealization
