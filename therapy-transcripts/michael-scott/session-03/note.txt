Subjective

Client arrived having completed the homework from session 2 (Performance Log), bringing 12 entries on index cards. He reported the exercise was difficult but illuminating. Key entries:

1. Monday: Performed a "twirl" when the office didn't respond to his greeting. Trigger: room not responding. He noted: "Performed because the room didn't respond."
2. Wednesday: Used a comedic voice when speaking with David Wallace (CFO). Wrote: "Performed because I need David to think I'm good at my job. If he thinks I'm funny, maybe he won't fire me."
3. Thursday: Turned off TV at home (unusual), felt "bad" within two minutes, texted Dwight a meme. Wrote: "Performed because the silence felt like being alone. If I'm not sending something to someone, nobody knows I'm here."

Client identified the pattern himself: most performances are triggered by feeling someone might "leave emotionally" — zone out or stop paying attention. He described the performance as "like a faucet — you have to keep turning it or the water stops."

After sharing the log, the clinician presented a formulation and proposed treatment plan. Client received it with visible emotion, stating: "Nobody's ever understood what's going on. I didn't even know what was going on until you said it." He explicitly acknowledged his urge to make a joke ("Can I get this plan laminated?") and chose not to — a notable moment of self-observation in real time.

Assessment

Session 3 completes the assessment phase. The client engaged substantively with the homework and demonstrated readiness for active treatment. The formulation presented:

Core belief: "If I'm not entertaining, people will leave me."
Origin: Father's departure (age 4-5), stepfather's emotional disengagement, mother's responsive engagement to performance.
Current manifestation: Performance as default interpersonal mode; inability to identify own needs; toleration of criticism in relationships (Jan) as the price of presence; sleep disturbance as the system's inability to regulate without an audience.

The client's homework revealed the performance pattern operates continuously — in the office, with authority figures, and in solitude. The Thursday entry (silence → anxiety → reaching out via phone) is particularly significant: it demonstrates that the performance compulsion extends beyond social contexts into self-regulation of affect.

The client's moment of choosing not to make a joke when receiving the treatment plan is a small but meaningful clinical signal: he is beginning to observe the impulse in real time, which is a prerequisite for change.
