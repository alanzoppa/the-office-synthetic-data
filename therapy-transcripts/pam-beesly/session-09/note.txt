Date: Week 9
Clinician: Dr. James Pierce, PsyD
Client: Pam Beesly
Session Type: Individual Therapy

Subjective

Client completed the "look at this" experiment with Oscar. Oscar looked at the watercolor for thirty seconds, gave specific, genuine feedback ("The light on the cars — you really captured the late afternoon sun" + constructive criticism about muddy foreground), and said "keep going." Client reported: "The 'that's nice, honey' didn't come. He saw the painting and he saw me." This is the first real-world disconfirmation of the pre-rejection pattern.

The session introduced assertiveness work (Goal 3). Key insights:
- "No" left her vocabulary entirely — she doesn't hear it as an option; "yes" is a reflex
- She identified herself as a "feelings manager" — managing Roy's disinterest, mother's moods, Michael's need to be liked, Jim's feelings — at the cost of her own comfort
- "I'm uncomfortable so everyone else can be comfortable"
- She connected "selfish" to her mother's voice: "Don't be selfish, Pam. Don't think only of yourself." Wanting, saying no, having boundaries, existing with preferences — all coded as "selfish"
- She recognized that "managing someone's comfort" isn't love — it's fear. "I'm afraid of their negative feelings. So I prevent them. And the prevention looks like love but it's actually self-protection."
- Key statement: "I want to be okay without making it conditional on everyone else being okay first."

Objective

- Appearance: Neat; confident; brought the watercolor to show Oscar (carried it in her bag all week)
- Behavior: Cooperative; animated; produced spontaneous clinical language ("feelings manager," "being okay without conditions")
- Mood: Energized; tired (from the insight, not from distress)
- Affect: Genuine; pride about Oscar; frustration when identifying the "feelings manager" role; relief when articulating "I'm tired of managing everyone's feelings"
- Speech: Clear; direct; no trail-offs
- Thought process: Linear; strong synthesis — connected mother's "selfish" framing to her inability to say no
- Insight: Strong — can identify the function of her compliance (managing others' feelings) and the cost (self-erasure)
- Safety: Denies SI/HI

Assessment

The Oscar experiment succeeded — the first real-world evidence that "look at this" doesn't lead to "that's nice, honey." The data is accumulating (client's own framing).

The "feelings manager" formulation is a major clinical development. It reframes her "easygoing/nice" identity as a role she plays to manage others' affect, not a personality trait. This is the Pam equivalent of Michael's "performance" — the mechanism she uses to stay safe. Where Michael performs to prevent abandonment, Pam manages to prevent disappointment.

The "selfish" voice is the mother's, identified in real time. The connection between "selfish" and the inability to say no is the cognitive core of the assertiveness work.
