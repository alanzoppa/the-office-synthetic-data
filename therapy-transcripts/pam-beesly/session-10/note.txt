Date: Week 10
Clinician: Dr. James Pierce, PsyD
Client: Pam Beesly
Session Type: Individual Therapy

Subjective

Client completed the "say no" homework. Michael asked her to cover phones; she declined ("I can't cover the phones this time. I have a project I need to finish."). Michael said "okay" and asked Dwight. No negative consequences. Client reported: "Like I'd just jumped off a building and landed on a trampoline."

Key insight: she was pre-managing Michael's feelings about no before he even had them. "I was saying yes because I predicted he'd be upset by no. And he wasn't." She connected this to her mother's "are you sure?" — learning that no would be met with disapproval, so she pre-empted by never saying it.

New insight: she identified Michael as having the same fear (rejection) with a different strategy (performance vs. management). "Same fear, different volume." This is the first time she's recognized the pattern in someone else — a sign of metacognitive growth.

On her art: "The painting takes up the space the voice used to fill. And the painting is mine. The voice is hers. And mine is bigger than hers now." She described somatic change: "When I paint, my hands are steady. When the mother's voice was in charge, my hands shook. The shaking was hers. The steadiness is mine."

Client identified the "no" as the biggest therapeutic action so far: "Saying no was the thing I was most afraid of. And I did it. And I'm still here."

Objective

- Behavior: Cooperative; animated; produced spontaneous clinical insight about Michael
- Mood: Relieved; empowered
- Affect: Genuine pride; excitement about the "no" success; warmth about the art

- Insight: Strong — can see that pre-managing is based on a prediction (mother's pattern) not reality; can distinguish her body's response (steadiness) from the mother's (shaking)
- Safety: Denies SI/HI

Assessment

The "no" experiment is the Pam equivalent of Michael's break room experiment — the first real-world disconfirmation of the core avoidance pattern. The experience was significant enough that the client identified it as the most important moment in therapy, more than the painting or the want list.

The Michael insight ("same fear, different volume") is remarkable — it demonstrates that the client can now see the avoidance pattern as a human pattern, not just her personal deficiency. This is the beginning of self-compassion through universalization.

The somatic data (steady hands = her, shaking hands = mother's voice) is clinically valuable as a bodily marker the client can use to track whose voice is running.
