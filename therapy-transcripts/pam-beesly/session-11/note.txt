Date: Week 11
Clinician: Dr. James Pierce, PsyD
Client: Pam Beesly
Session Type: Individual Therapy

Subjective

Client brought the night parking lot watercolor. The therapist identified it as a painting about "being alone and not minding it." Client confirmed: she painted it at midnight when Roy was asleep and felt peaceful — not lonely — for the first time she can remember. "I was the main character in my own scene."

Assertiveness progress: said no to Angela (who routinely delegates her own spreadsheet work to Pam). Angela made a face but did the work herself. Client recognized: "She was using me. And I was letting her because saying no felt more dangerous than being used."

Major insight: "Calling things by their nice names." Client identified her pattern of labeling negative dynamics with positive words:
- Angela's taking advantage → "being helpful"
- Roy's disengagement → "being easygoing"
- Mother's control → "being supportive"

She described the nice names as "the same as 'fine' — the same defense." She practiced naming things accurately: "Angela takes advantage. Roy is disengaged. My mother is controlling. And I'm allowed to say that." The clinician noted these are descriptions, not condemnations.

Client reported the experience of removing the "easygoing costume": "The costume was warm but it was tight. I couldn't breathe in it. I can breathe now."

Objective

- Appearance: Neat; brought the night parking lot watercolor
- Behavior: Cooperative; calm; grounded; produced clinical insight without prompting ("nice names" pattern)
- Mood: Free; clear
- Affect: Genuine; peace about the midnight painting; determination about the naming; warmth about "a little brave"
- Speech: Clear; direct; no qualifiers
- Thought process: Linear; strong integrative capacity — connected the nice-naming to the "fine" defense
- Insight: Strong — can identify the function of euphemistic labeling and distinguish description from condemnation
- Safety: Denies SI/HI

Assessment

The "nice names" insight is a significant cognitive development. The client has been applying euphemistic labels to negative dynamics as a defense — the same function as "fine" (closing the door to honest assessment). Recognizing this pattern and practicing accurate naming is the cognitive defusion work (Goal 2) applied to interpersonal dynamics.

The night parking lot painting is the client's most authentic creative work — produced alone, at midnight, for no one. The therapist's reading ("a portrait of a feeling — being alone and not minding it") confirms the painting is not just art but self-representation: she's depicting a state of being that therapy has enabled.

Plan

1. Homework: Notice the "nice-naming" impulse. Let the real word exist next to the nice word. Don't have to say it aloud — just let it be there.
2. Next session: Session 12 — Jim, more honestly. The therapist asks "what does it feel like when he sees you?"
3. Note: The "nice names" insight should be connected to the Roy work — she's been calling his disengagement "fine" and "easygoing" for eight years
4. Note: The client is now consistently producing sharper insights than the therapist. The therapeutic relationship has shifted from directed to collaborative.
